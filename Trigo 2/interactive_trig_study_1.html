§0 Recognizing question types — what do I do first?

This is the section that actually wins you marks under time pressure. Knowing every formula doesn't help if you can't tell which formula a question is fishing for. Every question type below has a giveaway "look" — train yourself to spot the look and fire off the matching first move before you touch any algebra. Every §1–§8 section repeats this same idea in its own method box, but here they're all side by side so you can drill the recognition skill on its own.

The recognition table — surface feature → first move

How to use this tableCover the right-hand column with your hand. Read only the left column and say the first move out loud before checking. That's the actual skill being tested in an exam — not whether you know the formula, but whether you recognise when to reach for it.

§1 General Angles, ASTC & the Basic-Angle Method

Picture a line \(OA\) lying along the positive x-axis. Rotate it anticlockwise about \(O\) until it becomes a new line \(OP\). The angle swept out, \(\theta\), is the general angle — it can be anything at all: \(200°\), \(480°\), \(-150°\). The basic angle \(\alpha\) is always the acute angle that \(OP\) makes with the x-axis — the "shadow" of \(\theta\) back onto the nearest part of the axis.

Definition for any angle \[ \sin\theta = \frac{y}{r} \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{x}{r} \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \]

where \(P(x,y)\) lies on \(OP\) and \(r=OP>0\). This works even for obtuse, reflex, or negative angles — no right-angled triangle required.

1.1 Basic angle by quadrant, and the ASTC sign rule

Basic angle formula
  1. Quadrant 1 (\(0°\)–\(90°\)): \(\alpha=\theta\)
  2. Quadrant 2 (\(90°\)–\(180°\)): \(\alpha=180°-\theta\)
  3. Quadrant 3 (\(180°\)–\(270°\)): \(\alpha=\theta-180°\)
  4. Quadrant 4 (\(270°\)–\(360°\)): \(\alpha=360°-\theta\)

Since \(r>0\) always, only \(x\) and \(y\) change sign — so the sign of each ratio depends purely on the quadrant:

Quadrant\(\sin\theta\)\(\cos\theta\)\(\tan\theta\)Positive ratio
1 (\(0°\)–\(90°\))+++All
2 (\(90°\)–\(180°\))+−−Sine
3 (\(180°\)–\(270°\))−−+Tangent
4 (\(270°\)–\(360°\))−+−Cosine

1.2 Negative angles

A negative angle is measured clockwise from the positive x-axis instead. Reflecting \(P\) across the x-axis flips the sign of \(y\) only, which gives:

Negative-angle rules \[ \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta \qquad \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta \qquad \tan(-\theta)=-\tan\theta \]

Cosine is even; sine and tangent are odd. The reciprocal ratios inherit the same parity: \(\sec\) is even, \(\operatorname{cosec}\) and \(\cot\) are odd.

Try it before you look
Worked example — combining ASTC with a negative angle

Evaluate \(\tan 300° \times \cos(-150°)\) without a calculator.

1\(\tan300°\): \(300°\) is in Quadrant 4 (\(270°\)–\(360°\)), where tan is negative. Basic angle \(=360°-300°=60°\), so \(\tan300°=-\tan60°=-\sqrt3\).
2\(\cos(-150°)\): cosine is even, so \(\cos(-150°)=\cos150°\). \(150°\) is Quadrant 2, basic angle \(=180°-150°=30°\), cos negative there: \(\cos150°=-\cos30°=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).
3Multiply the two results: \((-\sqrt3)\times\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt3\times\sqrt3}{2}=\dfrac{3}{2}\).
4Answer: \(\dfrac{3}{2}\).
Common trapNever read the quadrant off an angle bigger than \(360°\) (or smaller than \(0°\)) directly. \(\sin750°\) is not evaluated at \(750°\) — reduce it first: \(750°-720°=30°\), then proceed as normal.

§2 Reciprocal & Complementary Ratios

Reciprocal ratios \[ \operatorname{cosec}\theta=\frac{1}{\sin\theta} \qquad \sec\theta=\frac{1}{\cos\theta} \qquad \cot\theta=\frac{1}{\tan\theta}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} \]

Each one's sign follows automatically from the ratio it's the reciprocal of — \(\operatorname{cosec}\theta\) is positive exactly when \(\sin\theta\) is positive.

Complementary angles \[ \sin(90°-\theta)=\cos\theta \qquad \cos(90°-\theta)=\sin\theta \qquad \tan(90°-\theta)=\cot\theta \]

True for any general angle \(\theta\), not just acute ones — the "co-" in cosine literally means "sine of the complement."

Try it before you look
Worked example — one given ratio, several asked for

Given \(\tan A=-\tfrac{5}{12}\) with \(A\) in Quadrant 2, find \(\sec A\) and \(\cos(90°-A)\).

1Ignore the sign and build a right triangle from the magnitude \(\tfrac{5}{12}\): this is a 5-12-13 triangle, so the hypotenuse is \(13\).
2In Quadrant 2, cosine is negative: \(\cos A=-\tfrac{12}{13}\), so \(\sec A=\dfrac{1}{\cos A}=-\dfrac{13}{12}\).
3\(\cos(90°-A)=\sin A\) by the complementary rule. In Quadrant 2, sine is positive: \(\sin A=\tfrac{5}{13}\).
4Final answers: \(\sec A=-\dfrac{13}{12}\), \(\cos(90°-A)=\dfrac{5}{13}\).
Key insightReciprocal ratios inherit their odd/even behaviour from the ratio they came from: \(\operatorname{cosec}\) is odd (like \(\sin\)), \(\sec\) is even (like \(\cos\)), \(\cot\) is odd (like \(\tan\)).

§3 Fundamental (Pythagorean) Identities

Take a right triangle with legs \(a,b\), hypotenuse \(c\), angle \(\theta\) opposite \(a\). Pythagoras gives \(a^2+b^2=c^2\); dividing through by \(c^2\), then by \(b^2\), then by \(a^2\), produces all three identities below.

The three Pythagorean identities \[ \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1 \qquad \sec^2\theta=1+\tan^2\theta \qquad \operatorname{cosec}^2\theta=1+\cot^2\theta \]

True for every angle \(\theta\). Know every rearrangement too — e.g. \(\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta\), \(\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta-1\) — because you'll need whichever one fits the question.

Method — proving an identity
  1. Start on the side that looks more complicated (more terms, fractions, mixed angles). You rarely touch the simpler side.
  2. Expand brackets, combine fractions over a common denominator, or factorise.
  3. Substitute a Pythagorean identity wherever you see \(\sin^2+\cos^2\), \(\sec^2-\tan^2\), or \(\operatorname{cosec}^2-\cot^2\) — they always collapse to \(1\).
  4. If stuck, rewrite everything in terms of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) only.
Try it before you look
Worked example — proving an identity

Prove that \(\sec x-\cos x\equiv \sin x\tan x\).

1Start from the LHS (two unlike terms vs. a single product on the RHS): \(\sec x-\cos x=\dfrac{1}{\cos x}-\cos x\).
2Combine over a common denominator: \(=\dfrac{1-\cos^2x}{\cos x}\).
3Substitute the Pythagorean identity \(1-\cos^2x=\sin^2x\): \(=\dfrac{\sin^2x}{\cos x}=\sin x\cdot\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\).
4\(=\sin x\tan x\), which is the RHS. \(\checkmark\)
Reusable trickGiven \(\sin A\pm\cos A=k\), squaring both sides unlocks \(\sin A\cos A\) via \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\). Given \(\sec A+\tan A=k\), pair it with \((\sec A-\tan A)(\sec A+\tan A)=1\) and add/subtract the two equations to solve for \(\sec A\) and \(\tan A\) separately.

§4 Solving Equations with Identities

Method
  1. Spot which functions are mixed, and pick a Pythagorean identity that converts one into the other.
  2. Substitute and simplify into a polynomial equation in one function.
  3. Solve that polynomial (factorise, or use the quadratic formula).
  4. Reject any value outside that function's valid range — remember \(|\sec x|\ge1\) and \(|\operatorname{cosec}x|\ge1\) always, and \(-1\le\sin x,\cos x\le1\).
  5. For each remaining value, find every angle in the given domain — check degrees vs radians, and don't miss the endpoints.
Try it before you look
Worked example — full equation solve

Solve \(2\tan^2x-3\sec x+3=0\) for \(0\le x\le2\pi\).

1Both \(\tan\) and \(\sec\) appear — use \(\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1\): \(2(\sec^2x-1)-3\sec x+3=0\).
2Simplify to a quadratic in \(\sec x\): \(2\sec^2x-3\sec x+1=0\).
3Factorise: \((2\sec x-1)(\sec x-1)=0 \Rightarrow \sec x=\tfrac12\) or \(\sec x=1\).
4Reject \(\sec x=\tfrac12\) — impossible, since \(|\sec x|\ge1\) always. Keep \(\sec x=1\Rightarrow\cos x=1\).
5In \([0,2\pi]\), \(\cos x=1\) at both ends of the domain: \(x=0,\ 2\pi\).
Key insightSeeing \(\sec^2x\) and \(\tan x\) together (or \(\operatorname{cosec}^2x\) and \(\cot x\)) is always your cue to substitute a Pythagorean identity and collapse to one function.

§5 Addition Formulae

The addition formulae \[ \sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B\pm\cos A\sin B \] \[ \cos(A\pm B)=\cos A\cos B\mp\sin A\sin B \] \[ \tan(A\pm B)=\dfrac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B} \]

Watch the cosine formula's sign — it flips relative to the left-hand side.

Common trap\(\sin(A+B)\ne\sin A+\sin B\). Trig functions never distribute over addition — always expand using the full formula.

5.1 Evaluating surds like \(\tan285°\)

Method
  1. Split the angle into a sum/difference of two special angles from \(\{30°,45°,60°,\dots\}\).
  2. Apply the relevant addition formula.
  3. Substitute special-angle values, using ASTC for any component beyond \(0°\)–\(90°\).
  4. Simplify the surd.
Try it before you look
Worked example — surd-form evaluation

Express \(\tan285°\) in surd form.

1Split: \(285°=240°+45°\), so \(\tan285°=\dfrac{\tan240°+\tan45°}{1-\tan240°\tan45°}\).
2\(\tan240°\): Quadrant 3, basic angle \(60°\), tan positive there: \(\tan240°=\tan60°=\sqrt3\).
3Substitute: \(\tan285°=\dfrac{\sqrt3+1}{1-\sqrt3}\).
4Rationalise by multiplying by \(\dfrac{1+\sqrt3}{1+\sqrt3}\): \(\dfrac{(\sqrt3+1)(1+\sqrt3)}{(1-\sqrt3)(1+\sqrt3)}=\dfrac{4+2\sqrt3}{-2}=-2-\sqrt3\).

§6 Quadrants, Compound Ratios & Proofs

Two very common question shapes: (a) you're given ratios of \(A\) and \(B\) plus a combined range, and asked for a compound ratio like \(\cos(A-B)\); (b) you're asked to prove an identity built from addition formulae.

Try it before you look
Worked example — quadrant work + addition formulae

Given \(\sin A=\tfrac35\), \(\sin B=\tfrac{5}{13}\), both \(A\) and \(B\) between \(90°\) and \(270°\), find \(\cos(A-B)\) and \(\tan(A+B)\).

1The range \(90°\)–\(270°\) spans Q2 and Q3. Since both sines are positive, and sin is only positive in Q2 within that range, both angles are in Q2 — so cosine is negative for both.
23-4-5 triangle: \(\cos A=-\tfrac45\). 5-12-13 triangle: \(\cos B=-\tfrac{12}{13}\).
3\(\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B=\left(-\tfrac45\right)\left(-\tfrac{12}{13}\right)+\tfrac35\cdot\tfrac5{13}=\tfrac{48}{65}+\tfrac{15}{65}=\tfrac{63}{65}\).
4\(\tan A=-\tfrac34\), \(\tan B=-\tfrac5{12}\). Then \(\tan(A+B)=\dfrac{\tan A+\tan B}{1-\tan A\tan B}=\dfrac{-\tfrac9{12}-\tfrac5{12}}{1-\tfrac{15}{48}}=-\dfrac{56}{33}\).
Key insightGiven two ratios plus a combined range, always pin down the exact quadrant for each angle using every piece of given information together before assigning any signs — not one clue at a time.

§7 R-Formulae

An expression like \(2\sin x+3\cos x\) is a sum of two waves — but it always simplifies into a single wave \(R\sin(x+\alpha)\) with a new amplitude \(R\) and a phase shift \(\alpha\).

Derivation — compare coefficients

Suppose \(a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta\equiv R\sin(\theta+\alpha)\). Expand the right side: \(R\sin\theta\cos\alpha+R\cos\theta\sin\alpha\). Comparing coefficients: \(R\cos\alpha=a\), \(R\sin\alpha=b\). Divide to get \(\tan\alpha=\tfrac{b}{a}\); square and add to get \(R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).

The four R-formula cases (\(a,b>0\), \(R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), \(\tan\alpha=b/a\), \(\alpha\) acute) \[ a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta=R\sin(\theta+\alpha) \qquad a\sin\theta-b\cos\theta=R\sin(\theta-\alpha) \] \[ a\cos\theta+b\sin\theta=R\cos(\theta-\alpha) \qquad a\cos\theta-b\sin\theta=R\cos(\theta+\alpha) \]
Try it before you look
Worked example — R-formula from scratch

Express \(7\sin\theta-24\cos\theta\) as a single trig function.

1Pattern is \(a\sin\theta-b\cos\theta\), so the form is \(R\sin(\theta-\alpha)\), with \(a=7,\ b=24\).
2\(R=\sqrt{7^2+24^2}=\sqrt{625}=25\).
3\(\tan\alpha=\dfrac{24}{7}\Rightarrow\alpha\approx73.7°\).
4Answer: \(7\sin\theta-24\cos\theta=25\sin(\theta-73.7°)\).
Common trapKeep \(\alpha\) acute by insisting \(a,b>0\) before you start — this keeps the reference triangle in Quadrant 1, the easiest case for computing \(\tan\alpha\) and \(R\).

§8 Applications of R-Formulae

Method — solving \(a\sin\theta\pm b\cos\theta=c\)
  1. Convert the left side into R-form.
  2. You now have \(R\sin(\theta+\alpha)=c\), i.e. \(\sin(\theta+\alpha)=c/R\).
  3. Solve for \((\theta+\alpha)\) — remember to shift the domain by \(\alpha\) too, since \((\theta+\alpha)\) ranges over a different interval than \(\theta\) does.
  4. Subtract \(\alpha\) back off each solution, and discard anything outside the original domain.
Try it before you look
Worked example — solving with R-form

Solve \(8\sin x+\sqrt3\cos x=5\) for \(0°\le x\le360°\).

1\(R=\sqrt{64+3}=\sqrt{67}\approx8.185\), \(\tan\alpha=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{8}\Rightarrow\alpha\approx12.2°\). So the equation becomes \(\sqrt{67}\sin(x+12.2°)=5\).
2\(\sin(x+12.2°)=\dfrac{5}{\sqrt{67}}\approx0.611\). Since \(x\in[0°,360°]\), \((x+12.2°)\in[12.2°,372.2°]\).
3Basic angle for \(\sin(\cdot)=0.611\) is \(\approx37.7°\), giving \((x+12.2°)=37.7°\) or \(180°-37.7°=142.3°\) within range.
4Subtract \(12.2°\) back off: \(x\approx25.5°\) or \(x\approx130.1°\).
Maximum / minimum values

Because \(-1\le\sin(\cdot)\le1\) always, once written as \(R\sin(\theta+\alpha)\), the maximum is \(R\) (when \(\sin(\cdot)=1\)) and the minimum is \(-R\) (when \(\sin(\cdot)=-1\)) — no calculus needed.

Key insightSquaring a range that straddles zero, like \([-R,R]\), gives a new minimum of \(0\) — not \(R^2\) — because the original expression passes through zero somewhere in the domain. An odd power like cubing, by contrast, keeps the max and min in the same order.

§9 Exam cheat-sheet

Every formula, in one place \[ \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta,\quad \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta,\quad \tan(-\theta)=-\tan\theta \] \[ \sin(90°-\theta)=\cos\theta,\quad \cos(90°-\theta)=\sin\theta,\quad \tan(90°-\theta)=\cot\theta \] \[ \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1,\quad \sec^2\theta=1+\tan^2\theta,\quad \operatorname{cosec}^2\theta=1+\cot^2\theta \] \[ \sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B\pm\cos A\sin B \] \[ \cos(A\pm B)=\cos A\cos B\mp\sin A\sin B \] \[ \tan(A\pm B)=\dfrac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B} \] \[ a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta=R\sin(\theta+\alpha),\quad a\sin\theta-b\cos\theta=R\sin(\theta-\alpha) \] \[ a\cos\theta+b\sin\theta=R\cos(\theta-\alpha),\quad a\cos\theta-b\sin\theta=R\cos(\theta+\alpha) \]

where \(R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), \(\tan\alpha=b/a\), \(\alpha\) acute, \(a,b>0\).
